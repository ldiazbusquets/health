Algorithm Bias

A hospital trains a model to flag patients at risk from a blood marker and a symptom score. In group B, the marker reads lower for the same illness, much as some real devices read differently across patients. If the training data is mostly group A, the model learns group A's pattern.

Bias without intent

Nobody told this model to treat the groups differently. It learned one decision line from data that was mostly group A, and that line sits in the wrong place for group B.

Overall accuracy hides it

Because group A dominates, the model can look accurate overall while missing many sick patients in group B. Fairness checks report error rates for each group separately.

It happens in practice

A widely used US care-management algorithm underestimated Black patients' needs because it predicted cost rather than illness (Obermeyer et al., Science, 2019). Pulse oximeters missed low oxygen more often in Black patients (Sjoding et al., NEJM, 2020).

All patients here are synthetic: generated in your browser from made-up distributions. The model is a real logistic regression trained by gradient descent each time you change a setting. Sources: Obermeyer et al., Science (2019); Sjoding et al., NEJM (2020). Educational only; not medical advice.
